Eastern Front Model 1941–42

A month-by-month model of the German–Soviet war. Each month it carries forward men, tanks and aircraft on both sides, the German railheads and supply reach on three axes, and the Soviet production base, and moves the front by a dozen mechanisms. Its constants are set so that the real decisions reproduce the real front and the real losses of both years; the assumptions below then play the scenario. Results are the middle of 600 runs, with the 10th to 90th percentile where it matters.

How the model works
  • State. Each month: German men, tanks and aircraft in the east; Soviet field men, tanks and aircraft; the depth of the front on three axes (Leningrad, Moscow, the south, with the Caucasus as a branch); the railheads and supply reach on each; the Soviet production base.
  • Pace. The spearheads' pace is a calibrated open-season rate times the force ratio (a weighted mix of men, tanks and aircraft) to a small, uncertain power (about 0.3) in open country, where the spearheads moved as supply allowed whatever the odds, and to a large one (about 0.7) in a set-piece attack on a held front (Moscow beyond the Mozhaisk line in 1941; the attack of 1942), where force decides, times a season factor (mud in October, frost from November, the thaw in April), and it cannot go beyond the supply reach plus a short overrun. The reach is the railhead plus what lorries can bridge; the railheads follow the front at a limited rate and lag behind it (less in 1942, when the Dnieper bridges and the Donbas network were in use); lorries manage about a third as much in mud and snow, which is what halts Typhoon in the model; a division brings its own transport, so the levers for lorries and fuel are net of what the extra divisions use.
  • Decisions. After Smolensk the Centre recovers (Yelnya, replacements, stocks) and, if the Kiev diversion is on, sends its panzers south; Typhoon opens at the real date, brought forward by an uncertain share of an earlier Smolensk, and two to four weeks earlier without the diversion, at the cost of a flank to cover; beyond the Mozhaisk line the drive on Moscow is a set-piece attack against the Reserve Front and the Moscow defence zone, whose strength grows with the calendar and with Soviet mobilization; the south's slow start to the Dnieper is a calibrated factor, and at the Dnieper the South waits a calibrated month for the Kiev pocket to close (longer without the Centre's panzers, shorter if the Soviets withdraw, in which case the intact South-Western Front slows the advance on Kharkov and the Donbass instead, though Kleist, with no pocket to close and no Kremenchug thrust to make, drives east with his whole group, and the Centre must still cover a flank, if a retreating one), then reduces it for most of a month at half pace, and the North fights a calibrated month or so (uncertain by a fifth) for the Luga Line, as it did in reality, longer or shorter as the Red Army at that date is stronger or weaker than the real one (the divisions that reach the line in order, the mechanized corps, the KV deliveries), and the broad-front battle for a held line costs the defender killed and wounded rather than a pocket. In 1942 the German plan is the south alone (Blue, as in reality), Moscow first and then the south (the article), or Moscow alone, with the whole striking power on one axis, no Blue, and the November counter-offensive aimed at the salient's German-held flanks, which are seven-tenths of the army's power against four-tenths plus the allies on the Don (Mars on a larger scale, and it usually fails); Timoshenko's May offensive at Kharkov is a test of the southern balance against the real one, which failed at Barvenkovo: at about 1.5 × the real power ratio (uncertain between 1.2 and 1.8 — Fridericus had its anxious moments, but Barvenkovo was lost by deployment as much as by strength, and easier by 0.15 from a continuous Donets front with no salient to cut off) it succeeds, retakes Kharkov, throws the Germans back toward the Dnieper bend and delays Blue by four to eight weeks while the Sixth Army refits, otherwise it ends in the Izyum pocket as it did; under the two-step plan the Germans attack Moscow first, then the south; a set-piece attack on a held front moves at a fraction of the open pace, cut further by the Moscow defences, and is spent after one to two months if the city has not fallen. Blue cannot open until the Moscow attack is over and its panzer corps have come south: a week after success (the article's 26 June and 1 July), but an uncertain two to six weeks after failure, and then a tenth to three-tenths weaker for its first two months; a late Moscow therefore costs the south its summer. The Stavka, confirmed in its view that Moscow was the objective, moves no reserves south on the strength of a failed attack. Soviet choices: defence in depth, a fighting withdrawal on the Dvina and the Velikaya and a prepared line on the Dvina–Dnieper that hold for a set number of weeks and are then broken on a broad front (the railheads catch up meanwhile, and the Centre halts at Smolensk to recover whenever it gets there), withdrawing from Kiev on Zhukov's proposal (29 July: the line given up in August, the freed armies block Kleist and Guderian, the pocket three-tenths of the real) or Budyonny's (11 September: held as in reality, then abandoned; half get away), stopping the winter offensive in January, no Kharkov offensive. The drive on Tikhvin opens three weeks after Leningrad is invested, with what is left of the autumn, and is over by December; the siege bites only while Tikhvin is held.
  • Pockets. Prisoners follow the Soviet men present, the weighted advance, how far forward the Soviets stand, German sealing power (infantry and aircraft), and the alert's leakage in the first two months.
  • Attrition. The Soviet fighting-value lever multiplies Soviet combat power everywhere it enters (the force ratio, the exchange of losses, the winter regain, the November counter-offensive) and divides the pockets by its square root. In 1942 it also carries the cadres: the trained army of 1941 and its officers were lost in the pockets, so the fighting value per man moves by four-tenths of the proportional difference in 1941's irrecoverable losses from the real, the summer's losses (the cadre army: Uman, Kiev) counted in full and the autumn's reservists and militia at half — a 1942 Red Army that lost fewer of them fights better, one that lost more fights worse, within a fifth either way. Twelve full mechanized corps instead of thirty half-formed ones, if chosen, add a tenth through 1941. Soviet killed and wounded follow German combat power and the intensity of the month, more when the Red Army is attacking (the winter, May 1942, the second half of 1942); German casualties follow Soviet power and intensity. The Luftwaffe lever enters everywhere as its 0.6 power while the front is moving and its 0.85 power when it stands: forward airfields, fuel transport and ground crews set the sortie rate in the east, not the number of aircraft, and the ground organization lagged the panzers. Tanks and aircraft are lost as a share of stock each month, more in 1941 than 1942, plus the tanks left in pockets. The German tank stock follows its real curve: from 3,600 in June 1941 to about 2,000 by December, as a share of stock each month of battle (a defender on a static front loses fewer), then rebuilt through the winter and spring by the workshops' repairs and the refit for Blue to about 3,000 by June 1942, and worn down again to some 2,300 by the end of the year. The weather enters the air arm month by month: both sides' aircraft count half in the mud of October and November, the Luftwaffe's two-fifths and the Red Air Force's seven-tenths from December to February (the German ground organization was not built for a Russian winter; the Soviet one was), three-fifths and four-fifths in March, half each in the April thaw, and the same again in the November and December of 1942. A Red Army attacking through the winter and the thaw (December 1941 to April 1942) loses at nearly three times the rate of a defending one: frontal attacks over snow on dug-in positions, as the real quarter's 1.85 million against some 450,000 German shows. The Crimea is not an axis of the model, so its two battles of 1942 are entered as they happened in every run: the Kerch peninsula in May (170,000 prisoners) and the fall of Sevastopol in July (95,000). Blue's pockets in 1942 seal at half the rate of 1941's (the Stavka's order of 6 July to withdraw before encirclement), and the Sixth Army is held a calibrated fortnight in the great bend of the Don before it reaches the Volga. The Luftwaffe receives 250 aircraft a month in 1941 and 300 in 1942 against its losses, so that its eastern strength runs at about 2,500–2,750 through 1942 rather than growing. The outcome of each campaign then adds its own bill, on the pattern of the real battles. A Moscow that falls is a Kiev- or Vyazma-sized pocket round the capital: some 350,000 prisoners (the article's figure) and half as many killed and wounded, scaled by the Soviet strength in the field, at a cost to the Germans, beyond the months of full-intensity fighting the attack already carries, of 40,000. A Moscow attack that fails costs the Germans about as much (Rzhev, Kursk: the attacker bleeds on prepared defences and in the counter-attacks that follow), the defenders a third as much and almost no prisoners. Leningrad's starved garrison capitulates with the city (280,000 prisoners; Sevastopol and Singapore on a larger scale); Stalingrad's fall costs the remnant of the Sixty-second Army (40,000 prisoners, 30,000 killed and wounded); Astrakhan and Grozny a garrison each. A November counter-offensive that fails costs the Soviets some 150,000 (Operation Mars cost 215,000) instead of the Germans, who are spared the encirclement's losses (60,000).
  • Production and men. Soviet output follows the real monthly figures, moved by half the difference in territory lost (two-fifths less of it with a leadership alerted beforehand, which plans the evacuation and holds stocks) (the other half stands for evacuation and substitution; Moscow's factories are 7 per cent more); the evacuation ran only a few weeks ahead of the panzers (Zaporozhye in August–September, Kharkov's tank plant to 20 October, Moscow's works in October–November), so territory taken ahead of the real schedule has its plant caught in proportion to the lead, nothing under two weeks and the full extra weight at ten, and what is caught stays lost; mobilization follows the real inflow (an invasion on 1 June calls the same pools up three weeks earlier, so half of that first month's extra inflow is debited from 1942's, on both sides); Lend-Lease adds tanks and aircraft from October 1941; purchases for gold through Vladivostok instead (the scenario's America is neutral and sells for cash, Japan lets no arms through) bring trucks, aviation fuel, aluminium and rail stock from the spring of 1942, worth a little output, somewhat smaller pockets and a little reserve power on the Don. German replacements and arrivals follow the real flow, raised by the output and replacement levers.
  • Reserves. The Stavka's weight between the three axes is a choice (in reality the Kiev group of fronts in 1941, Moscow in 1942). An axis holding more than its real share is harder to attack, most of all by a set-piece attack, and yields smaller pockets; one holding less is easier. Moscow is also the hub of the railways: if it falls, the Stavka's capacity to move reserves between theatres drops to an uncertain half to four-fifths (the Volga bypass lines were fewer and slower), half of the loss made good over a year. Its factories are a further 7 per cent of pre-war output lost (the region made about a fifth of Soviet industry, but much of it had gone east in the autumn of 1941). Fewer reserves then reach the south, which raises the pressure on Stalingrad and cuts the reserves that can be brought to the Don for the November counter-offensive; the winter counter-offensive of 1941–42, whose reserve armies formed around Moscow, is weakened likewise.
  • Weather. How much of October and November 1941 and of the spring of 1942 is lost to mud is drawn for each run, for reality too, so the real settings now give a spread of outcomes around the calibrated run rather than a single one; the calibrated run is the middle of that spread.
  • The winter. The Soviet counter-offensive regains ground on the Moscow axis in proportion to the strength ratio. Tikhvin is retaken in December if the Soviets are strong enough relative to the real winter; if not, Leningrad has no route across Lake Ladoga and capitulates after about three or four months.
  • Stalingrad and Uranus. Once the Volga is reached, pressure on the city accumulates with the force ratio and air power; it falls if the pressure passes the real total, which did not suffice, by a margin. The November counter-offensive breaks through if the Soviet reserve power against the German flank reaches a share of the real ratio, which did suffice. The flank it strikes is four-tenths of the German army's power (the reserves behind the Romanian sectors, the panzer corps that can turn) plus the allied armies, the Romanian Third and Fourth, the Italian Eighth and the Hungarian Second, some 650,000 men counted at a third of a German formation's worth and unchanged by any lever: more German men thicken the flank, but only its German part. The counter-offensive's own bill is added to December's: in success the Soviets pay for the Stalingrad offensive's first six weeks and Operation Mars together (some 320,000) and the Germans for the Chir and Kotelnikovo battles and the pocket's first weeks (60,000); in failure the attacker alone pays (150,000; Mars cost 215,000). Once the city has fallen a motorized group dashes down the west bank and across the Kalmyk steppe for Astrakhan (the objective of Directive No. 45; in reality a division patrolled to within 40 km of it): there is no railway, so it runs on lorries beyond the Stalingrad railhead, at an uncertain pace, and in November the rains and the first frost cut both its pace and its reach by an uncertain half or so, so a city not reached by early November is usually not reached at all. The group is a tenth of the southern striking force, and when it has gone before the counter-offensive opens the Don flank is that much thinner. The Caucasus branch gets a settable share of the south's supply reach while Stalingrad is fought for (the rest feeds the city's attackers), and the whole of it once the city falls, with its own weather (a dry October, rain in November, the passes closed in December) and the coast road's lorry reach; it ends where the railhead's lag leaves it, about Derbent. Baku lies beyond the model's window, as it did beyond the winter.
  • Uncertainty. Each mechanism's strength is drawn from a range that acts only on departures from the real war, so the real settings always return the calibrated real run.

An independent review of this version found and I corrected: the Moscow defences had raised Soviet rather than German losses; the German losses of the Moscow attack were understated; the Centre's panzers were counted in the south and at Moscow at once in September; the Luftwaffe was counted twice at Stalingrad, and pressure went on accruing after November; Blue could open in May or never; one uncertainty range leaked into the real run. Calibration: the real run matches the real dates of Minsk, Smolensk, Vyazma, Kiev, Kharkov, Rostov, Daugavpils, Pskov, Shlisselburg, Tikhvin, Voronezh, the Volga, Maikop and Mozdok within a few days to three weeks; the Dec 1941 and 1942 depths, Soviet and German casualties, prisoners, tank and aircraft losses and strengths within about 12 per cent. Two judgements are not calibrated against anything: how far a 1942 set-piece attack on Moscow would get (set so that the real 1942 German army would succeed about half the time at the default defences), and the threshold at which Moscow falls in 1941 (80 km beyond the real halt). The model does not take Leningrad by assault, and it does not decide; the decisions are levers.
