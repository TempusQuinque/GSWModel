Eastern Front Model 1941–42

A month-by-month model of the German–Soviet war. Each month it carries forward men, tanks and aircraft on both sides, the German railheads and supply reach on three axes, and the Soviet production base, and moves the front by a dozen mechanisms. Its constants are set so that the real decisions reproduce the real front and the real losses of 1941 and 1942; the assumptions below then play the scenario. This edition runs from June 1941 to December 1942. Results are the middle of 600 runs, with the 10th to 90th percentile where it matters.

How the model works
  • State. Each month: German men, tanks and aircraft in the east; Soviet field men, tanks and aircraft; the depth of the front on three axes (Leningrad, Moscow, the south, with the Caucasus as a branch); the railheads and supply reach on each; the Soviet production base.
  • Pace. The spearheads' pace is a calibrated open-season rate times the force ratio (a weighted mix of men, tanks and aircraft) to a small, uncertain power (about 0.3) in open country, where the spearheads moved as supply allowed whatever the odds, and to a large one (about 0.7) in a set-piece attack on a held front (Moscow beyond the Mozhaisk line in 1941; the attack of 1942), where force decides, times a season factor (mud in October, frost from November, the thaw in April), and it cannot go beyond the supply reach plus a short overrun. The reach is the railhead plus what lorries can bridge; the railheads follow the front at a limited rate and lag behind it (less in 1942, when the Dnieper bridges and the Donbas network were in use); lorries manage about a third as much in mud and snow, which is what halts Typhoon in the model; a division brings its own transport, so the levers for lorries and fuel are net of what the extra divisions use. Fuel has a lever for each year: in 1941 the limit at the front was the railways and the lorry columns rather than the oil in the Reich; in 1942 the real shortage was absolute, and the 1942 lever's gain over 1941's (or over the real, if 1941's is below it) is multiplied in each run by an uncertain factor between 0.4 and 1.6, for how much of it reaches the front.
  • Decisions. After Smolensk the Centre recovers (Yelnya, replacements, stocks) and, if the Kiev diversion is on, sends its panzers south; Typhoon opens at the real date, brought forward by an uncertain share of an earlier Smolensk, and two to four weeks earlier without the diversion, at the cost of a flank to cover; beyond the Mozhaisk line the drive on Moscow is a set-piece attack against the Reserve Front and the Moscow defence zone, whose strength grows with the calendar and with Soviet mobilization; the south's slow start to the Dnieper is a calibrated factor over the first ten weeks of the war, and at the Dnieper the South waits a calibrated month for the Kiev pocket to close (longer without the Centre's panzers, shorter if the Soviets withdraw, in which case the intact South-Western Front slows the advance on Kharkov and the Donbass instead, though Kleist, with no pocket to close and no Kremenchug thrust to make, drives east with his whole group, and the Centre must still cover a flank, if a retreating one), then reduces it for most of a month at half pace, and the North fights a calibrated month or so (uncertain by a fifth) for the Luga Line, as it did in reality, longer or shorter as the Red Army at that date is stronger or weaker than the real one (the divisions that reach the line in order, the mechanized corps, the KV deliveries), and the broad-front battle for a held line costs the defender killed and wounded rather than a pocket. In 1942 the German plan is the south alone (Blue, as in reality), Moscow first and then the south (the article), or Moscow alone, with the whole striking power on one axis, no Blue, and the November counter-offensive aimed at the salient's German-held flanks, which are seven-tenths of the army's power against four-tenths plus the allies on the Don (Mars on a larger scale, and it usually fails); Timoshenko's May offensive at Kharkov is a test of the southern balance against the real one, which failed at Barvenkovo: at about 1.5 × the real power ratio (uncertain between 1.2 and 1.8 — Fridericus had its anxious moments, but Barvenkovo was lost by deployment as much as by strength, and easier by 0.15 from a continuous Donets front with no salient to cut off) it succeeds, retakes Kharkov, throws the Germans back toward the Dnieper bend and delays Blue by four to eight weeks while the Sixth Army refits, otherwise it ends in the Izyum pocket as it did; under the two-step plan the Germans attack Moscow first, then the south; a set-piece attack on a held front moves at a fraction of the open pace, cut further by the Moscow defences, and is spent after one to two months if the city has not fallen. Blue cannot open until the Moscow attack is over and its panzer corps have come south: a week after success (the article's 26 June and 1 July), but an uncertain two to six weeks after failure, and then a tenth to three-tenths weaker for its first two months; a late Moscow therefore costs the south its summer. The Stavka, confirmed in its view that Moscow was the objective, moves no reserves south on the strength of a failed attack. Soviet choices: defence in depth; defensive lines (see Lines, below: the railheads catch up while a line holds, and the Centre halts at Smolensk to recover whenever it gets there); withdrawing from Kiev on Zhukov's proposal (29 July: the line given up in August, the freed armies block Kleist and Guderian, the pocket three-tenths of the real) or Budyonny's (11 September: held as in reality, then abandoned; half get away); stopping the winter offensive in January; no Kharkov offensive. The drive on Tikhvin opens three weeks after Leningrad is invested, with what is left of the autumn, and is over by December; the siege bites only while Tikhvin is held.
  • Lines. Seven lines the Soviets could have prepared and manned in 1941 beyond what they did: the Dvina and the Velikaya (the Stalin Line at Ostrov and Pskov) on the road to Leningrad; the Minsk fortified region and the Dvina–Dnieper (Vitebsk, Orsha, Mogilev) on the road to Moscow; Dubno and Rovno, the Stalin Line in the Ukraine and the Dnieper below Kiev in the south. One control sets them all: none (as in 1941), very plausible (the Dvina thinly, the Dvina–Dnieper strongly, the Ukrainian Stalin Line thinly), plausible (all seven, the main ones strongly) or possible (all seven strongly); each can also be set on its own, not manned, thinly or strongly. They draw on one pool of second-echelon divisions: the real second strategic echelon, some 57 divisions, and one more for every 18,000 men in the west beyond the real 2.9 million and the echelon's own 670,000, half of it in place in time without the alert, as in 1941, when the reserve armies arrived piecemeal; if the lines chosen need more, every line is thinned in proportion. A line's days are worked out on the day it is reached: from the men on it (a thinly manned line holds about three-fifths as long); from the balance of forces that day, since a held line is a set-piece attack for the Germans (to the set-piece power, about 0.7, and with the Stavka's weight on the axis); and from the German supply, since an attacker with a full lorry reach in hand breaks a line in about two-thirds of the time one at the end of his supply needs, though the latter's wait mostly overlaps the pause he needed anyway; on the roads to Leningrad and Moscow the spearheads reach the lines at the end of their supply, in the south with it in hand, so the southern lines are broken sooner for the same men. The days come on top of what the real lines held, which the calibrated run already has. Strongly manned with the scenario's forces, they hold from about eight days to a fortnight: judgements set against the real cases (the Dnieper in July 1941 held about a week with half its armies arrived; Korosten held five weeks, the Kiev fortified region two months), uncertain by a quarter either way. Each line may give way early, after 15 to 45 per cent of its days, when bridges are taken intact, a flank is turned or a stand-fast order makes it a pocket: between a quarter and two-fifths of the time without the alert, a quarter less often if strongly manned, a quarter more at half strength and up to half as much again when thinner, and two-fifths less with the alert's prepared demolitions; its men are then cut off: twice what it would lose in killed and wounded on a broad front, as prisoners. The Velikaya is manned only as far as the Dvina buys the time, and its divisions, falling back in order, add up to three days on the Luga; a Dvina that holds adds up to four through the Soltsy counterattack. The Dnieper below Kiev is manned only as far as a stand at Dubno or on the Stalin Line has kept the armies of the south out of the Uman pocket (a line that gave way early buys three-tenths as much), and not with either Kiev withdrawal (Zhukov's gives the Dnieper up, Budyonny's comes when Kleist is already across). Held, the Dnieper below Kiev takes the Kiev pocket's southern pincer. While a line holds, the Germans lose men at 1.3 times the month's rate on that axis. None of these lines was held in reality, so the real run is unchanged.
  • Pockets. Prisoners follow the Soviet men present, the weighted advance, how far forward the Soviets stand, German sealing power (infantry and aircraft), and the alert's leakage in the first 40 days. The Dnieper below Kiev, if it is held and does not give way early, leaves the Kiev pocket without its southern pincer: the southern advance of the three weeks after the Kiev line breaks yields six-tenths of the pocket it would otherwise give if the line is strongly manned, and a smaller cut if thinly. A line that gives way early has its men cut off (see Lines). Within 1941 the prisoners come somewhat early: the year's total matches the real one, but the frontier battles take too many and Kiev too few, so whatever acts in the first weeks (the alert, the frontier lines) works on too large a number.
  • Attrition. The Soviet fighting-value lever multiplies Soviet combat power everywhere it enters (the force ratio, the exchange of losses, the winter regain, the November counter-offensive) and divides the pockets by its square root. Experience (below) moves each side's fighting value per man in the same places. Twelve full mechanized corps instead of thirty half-formed ones, if chosen, add a tenth through 1941. Soviet killed and wounded follow German combat power and the intensity of the month, more when the Red Army is attacking (the winter, May 1942, the second half of 1942); German casualties follow Soviet power and intensity. The Luftwaffe lever enters everywhere as its 0.6 power while the front is moving and its 0.85 power when it stands: forward airfields, fuel transport and ground crews set the sortie rate in the east, not the number of aircraft, and the ground organization lagged the panzers. Tanks and aircraft are lost as a share of stock each month, more in 1941 than 1942, plus the tanks left in pockets. The German tank stock follows its real curve: from 3,600 in June 1941 to about 2,000 by December, as a share of stock each month of battle (a defender on a static front loses fewer), then rebuilt through the winter and spring by the workshops' repairs and the refit for Blue to about 3,000 by June 1942, and worn down again to some 2,300 by the end of the year. The weather enters the air arm month by month: both sides' aircraft count half in the mud of October and November, the Luftwaffe's two-fifths and the Red Air Force's seven-tenths from December to February (the German ground organization was not built for a Russian winter; the Soviet one was), three-fifths and four-fifths in March, half each in the April thaw, and the same again in the November and December of 1942. A Red Army attacking through the winter and the thaw (December 1941 to April 1942) loses at nearly three times the rate of a defending one: frontal attacks over snow on dug-in positions, as the real quarter's 1.85 million against some 450,000 German shows. The Crimea is not an axis of the model, so its two battles of 1942 are entered as they happened in every run: the Kerch peninsula in May (170,000 prisoners) and the fall of Sevastopol in July (95,000). Blue's pockets in 1942 seal at half the rate of 1941's (the Stavka's order of 6 July to withdraw before encirclement), and the Sixth Army is held a calibrated fortnight in the great bend of the Don before it reaches the Volga. The Luftwaffe receives 250 aircraft a month in 1941 and 300 in 1942 against its losses, so that its eastern strength runs at about 2,500–2,750 through 1942 rather than growing. The outcome of each campaign then adds its own bill, on the pattern of the real battles, which enters the year's losses and, where it departs from the real war's, comes off the armies' strength (the real Kerch and Sevastopol are already in the calibrated strengths). A Moscow that falls is a Kiev- or Vyazma-sized pocket round the capital: some 350,000 prisoners (the article's figure) and half as many killed and wounded, scaled by the Soviet strength in the field, at a cost to the Germans, beyond the months of full-intensity fighting the attack already carries, of 40,000. A Moscow attack that fails costs the Germans about as much (Rzhev, Kursk: the attacker bleeds on prepared defences and in the counter-attacks that follow), the defenders a third as much and almost no prisoners. Leningrad's starved garrison capitulates with the city (280,000 prisoners; Sevastopol and Singapore on a larger scale); Stalingrad's fall costs the remnant of the Sixty-second Army (40,000 prisoners, 30,000 killed and wounded); Astrakhan and Grozny a garrison each. A November counter-offensive that fails costs the Soviets some 150,000 (Operation Mars cost 215,000) instead of the Germans, who are spared the encirclement's losses (60,000).
  • Production and men. Soviet output follows the real monthly figures, moved by half the difference in territory lost (the other half stands for evacuation and substitution; a leadership alerted beforehand, which plans the evacuation and holds stocks, loses two-fifths less of what is lost by the end of 1941; Moscow's factories are 7 per cent more); the evacuation ran only a few weeks ahead of the panzers (Zaporozhye in August–September, Kharkov's tank plant to 20 October, Moscow's works in October–November), so territory taken ahead of the real schedule has its plant caught in proportion to the lead, nothing under two weeks and the full extra weight at ten, and what is caught while the front advances stays lost; mobilization follows the real inflow, less the second echelon's armies if they already stand in the west at the start, since in the real war they arrived in July and August (an invasion on 1 June calls the same pools up three weeks earlier, so half of that first month's extra inflow is debited from 1942's, on both sides); Lend-Lease adds tanks and aircraft from October 1941; purchases for gold through Vladivostok instead (the scenario's America is neutral and sells for cash, Japan lets no arms through) bring machine tools, aluminium, aviation spirit and some grain from the spring of 1942, worth a little output, but no lorries or rail stock. German replacements and arrivals follow the real flow, raised by the output and replacement levers.
  • Reserves. The Stavka's weight between the three axes is a choice (in reality the Kiev group of fronts in 1941, Moscow in 1942). An axis holding more than its real share is harder to attack, most of all by a set-piece attack, and yields smaller pockets; one holding less is easier. Moscow is also the hub of the railways: if it falls, the Stavka's capacity to move reserves between theatres drops to an uncertain half to four-fifths (the Volga bypass lines were fewer and slower), half of the loss made good over a year. Its factories are a further 7 per cent of pre-war output lost (the region made about a fifth of Soviet industry, but much of it had gone east in the autumn of 1941). Fewer reserves then reach the south, which raises the pressure on Stalingrad and cuts the reserves that can be brought to the Don for the November counter-offensive; the winter counter-offensive of 1941–42, whose reserve armies formed around Moscow, is weakened likewise.
  • Weather. How much of October and November 1941 and of the spring of 1942 is lost to mud is drawn for each run, for reality too, so the real settings now give a spread of outcomes around the calibrated run rather than a single one; the calibrated run is the middle of that spread.
  • The winter. The Soviet counter-offensive regains ground on the Moscow axis in proportion to the strength ratio. Tikhvin is retaken in December if the Soviets are strong enough relative to the real winter; if not, Leningrad has no route across Lake Ladoga and capitulates after about three or four months.
  • Stalingrad and Uranus. Once the Volga is reached, pressure on the city accumulates with the force ratio and air power; it falls if the pressure passes the real total, which did not suffice, by a margin. The November counter-offensive breaks through if the Soviet reserve power against the German flank reaches a share of the real ratio, which did suffice. The flank it strikes is four-tenths of the German army's power (the reserves behind the Romanian sectors, the panzer corps that can turn) plus the allied armies, the Romanian Third and Fourth, the Italian Eighth and the Hungarian Second, some 650,000 men counted at a third of a German formation's worth and unchanged by any lever: more German men thicken the flank, but only its German part. The counter-offensive's own bill is added to December's: in success the Soviets pay for the Stalingrad offensive's first six weeks and Operation Mars together (some 320,000) and the Germans for the Chir and Kotelnikovo battles and the pocket's first weeks (60,000); in failure the attacker alone pays (150,000; Mars cost 215,000). Once the city has fallen a motorized group dashes down the west bank and across the Kalmyk steppe for Astrakhan (the objective of Directive No. 45; in reality a division patrolled to within 40 km of it): there is no railway, so it runs on lorries beyond the Stalingrad railhead, at an uncertain pace, and in November the rains and the first frost cut both its pace and its reach by an uncertain half or so, so a city not reached by early November is usually not reached at all. The group is a tenth of the southern striking force, and when it has gone before the counter-offensive opens the Don flank is that much thinner. The Caucasus branch gets a settable share of the south's supply reach while Stalingrad is fought for (the rest feeds the city's attackers), and the whole of it once the city falls, with its own weather (a dry October, rain in November, the passes closed in December) and the coast road's lorry reach; beyond Grozny, in the Derbent gap between the mountains and the sea, it moves at about a third of the open pace; it ends where the railhead's lag leaves it, about Derbent.
  • Experience. Each army carries three measures per man, moved every month by its own losses and new men. Leaders, the officers and NCOs by number and training (the German army of 1940 = 1, the Red Army of June 1941 0.75): they fall at 1.6 times their men's rate in the fighting and with their men in the pockets (the pockets of June to September 1941, which took the first echelon's regular divisions rather than the reservists forming in the rear, took leaders and seasoned men at 1.2 times their share); new formations get the schools' leaders (Soviet 0.45 in 1941 and 0.55 in 1942; German 0.85 and 0.75), and seasoned men are promoted into the gaps. Seasoned men, those who have come through their first weeks of combat (5 per cent of the Red Army in June 1941, 70 per cent of the German army): the green die at one and a half times the rate, a month of heavy fighting seasons a quarter of the green who survive it, and replacements arrive partly seasoned, the German a quarter (men back from hospital, drafts trained in the field replacement battalions), the Soviet a twentieth. Learning, the army's as an institution (doctrine, staff work, the handling of tanks, guns and aircraft together): it grows with the fighting, as fast as the army keeps commanders who have fought and lived, toward three-fifths more for the Red Army and a tenth more for the German. Fighting value per man is leaders to an uncertain power of 0.3 to 0.6, times one plus 0.15 to 0.35 times the seasoned share, times learning to a power of 0.6 to 1. It multiplies each side's combat power (for the Red Army wherever the fighting-value lever enters), and a less experienced army also loses more men for the same enemy power and, on the German side, closes fewer pockets. In the real war this puts the Red Army at 0.90 of its June 1941 value per man by December 1941 (the cadre army lost) and 1.13 by December 1942, and the German army at 0.99 and 0.96: the German advantage per man grows by a tenth in 1941, and by the end of 1942 it is about a seventh below its level of June 1941. Like everything else it enters relative to the real war's own path. The men above the real June 1941 strengths come in at their source's quality: the first 670,000 extra Soviet men, the second echelon's armies, at the regular army's quality, their leaders taken off the inflow that would have brought them; the reservists beyond them unblooded and with the schools' leaders; the extra German divisions, from the West and the occupation forces, a little below the invasion army. Experience replaces the cadre rule of earlier versions, which counted the Red Army's 1941 losses in absolute numbers: per man, a larger army that loses more men is not for that reason a worse one.
  • What is left out. The model counts material as men, tanks, aircraft and lorries, and as supply reach; it does not count how well each is supplied in the field. The peace eased German shortages that it leaves out: spare parts and engines for the panzer divisions, tyres, tungsten-cored anti-tank shot (one of the few answers to the T-34 and KV in 1941), the aircrew the Luftwaffe kept by not fighting over Britain in 1940, and the fuel that the German mobile reserves behind the Don lacked in November 1942 (fuel enters the model only through the supply reach). It also leaves out the shortage of shells that a greater loss of factories would bring the Red Army (the model cuts only its tanks and aircraft), and the lorries and radios that Lend-Lease brought the real Red Army from late 1942, which the scenario's Red Army lacks. All of these favour Germany in the scenario, so in this respect the model, if anything, understates the German side.
  • Uncertainty. Each mechanism's strength is drawn from a range that acts only on departures from the real war, so the real settings always return the calibrated real run.

An independent review of this version found and I corrected: the Moscow defences had raised Soviet rather than German losses; the German losses of the Moscow attack were understated; the Centre's panzers were counted in the south and at Moscow at once in September; the Luftwaffe was counted twice at Stalingrad, and pressure went on accruing after November; Blue could open in May or never; one uncertainty range leaked into the real run. Calibration: the real run matches the real dates of Minsk, Smolensk, Vyazma, Kiev, Kharkov, Rostov, Daugavpils, Pskov, Shlisselburg, Tikhvin, Voronezh, the Volga, Maikop and Mozdok within a few days to three weeks; the Dec 1941 and 1942 depths, Soviet and German casualties, prisoners, tank and aircraft losses and strengths within about 12 per cent. Two judgements are not calibrated against anything: how far a 1942 set-piece attack on Moscow would get (set so that the real 1942 German army would succeed about half the time at the default defences), and the threshold at which Moscow falls in 1941 (80 km beyond the real halt). The model does not take Leningrad by assault, and it does not decide; the decisions are levers. Version 73 brought in experience in place of the cadre rule; the city battles' losses of 1941–42 (Moscow encircled, the garrisons of Leningrad and Stalingrad, the Crimea), which had entered only the year's losses, now come off the armies' strength too where they depart from the real war's; the fighting-value lever now counts in the November reserves, as the attrition note had always said; and the gold purchases no longer bring lorries or rail stock. The real run is unchanged. The scenario no longer has its week on the Dvina, so its lines are as in 1941 (Leningrad now capitulates in about three runs in five rather than a little over half). Version 76 gives the fuel of 1942 a lever of its own. In 1941 the limit at the front was the railways and the lorry columns, so the scenario keeps 1.05; by 1942 the real shortage was absolute, and for the scenario's Germany, trading freely overseas, the lever is set about a fifth higher, at the consumption of 1941; how much of that gain reaches the front is uncertain. It tells in the Caucasus and hardly elsewhere: the 1942 attack on Moscow is a set-piece battle that fuel does not decide. The gain in depth is small (a median of about 7 km by December, about 10 km in September), but Grozny is reached in about three runs in four rather than two in three, and by the end of September in more than half rather than about a third, because in a quarter of the runs the branch's supply had left it within 10 km of the town. Version 76 also corrects the Derbent gap, which the Caucasus branch had ignored in the month it passed Grozny. The calibrated real run is unchanged. Version 77 corrects faults found in an audit and an independent review. The scenario's extra 800,000 Soviet men in the west are chiefly the armies brought forward from the interior in May, the second echelon, which in the real war reached the front in July and August: the first 670,000 of them no longer arrive a second time, come in at the regular army's quality with their own leaders, and add nothing to the pool for the lines, which already counts them. What belongs to the first weeks of the war (the alert's slower frontier battles and leakier pockets, the first strike on the airfields, the south's slow start, defence in depth) is measured in days from the invasion, as with the real 22 June, rather than in calendar months, which an invasion on 1 June had stretched by three weeks. The alert's planned evacuation relieves only the output lost by the end of 1941, not Moscow's works in 1942; plant is caught only while the front advances; and the Soviet output lever acts from 1942, as labelled. The scenario now gives the article's 1942 in nearly every run: Moscow falls in nine runs in ten (one in seven in 1941), Leningrad and Stalingrad in all but a few, and the Caucasus no longer needs a larger share of the south's supply than in reality to reach Grozny about the article's date. With the very plausible lines Moscow still falls in 1942 in about half the runs, and Stalingrad in about three-quarters.
