Eastern Front Model 1941–42

The real campaign is the baseline. Each change the scenario makes acts through a stated mechanism whose strength is uncertain, so every result is a range from 1,500 runs: the middle figure, with the 10th to 90th percentile below it. With every assumption set to reality, the model returns the real figures.

How the model works
  • Baseline. Krivosheev's 1941 operations by phase (frontier, summer, autumn) and sector (Army Groups North, Centre, South), with their losses, durations and depths; Halder's German casualties; Bekker's Luftwaffe losses; Soviet tank stocks of 22,600 (June 1941) and 7,700 (January 1942).
  • Pace. Each phase's speed is the German-to-Soviet combat power ratio, relative to reality, raised to an uncertain power, times a readiness factor while the Soviets are alerted (slower frontier battles). Autumn progress slows in the mud (10 October–15 November) and again in the frost, and stops at the supply cap.
  • Pockets. Prisoners scale with the Soviet men present, with German sealing power (infantry and the Luftwaffe), and with how fast the pincers close, and are divided by a leak factor while the Soviets are alerted.
  • Fighting losses. Soviet killed and wounded scale with Soviet strength and with the length of each phase; men who escape a pocket partly become casualties breaking out. German casualties follow Soviet killed and wounded, raised by Soviet readiness and lowered by air support.
  • Equipment. Soviet tanks lost follow the tanks engaged; guns follow pockets and fighting; aircraft follow Luftwaffe strength, with fewer destroyed on the ground when alerted. German tanks and aircraft follow Soviet opposition and the Luftwaffe's own size.
  • Winter. Soviet strike power and German defence are each a weighted product of men, tanks and aircraft relative to reality. Soviet losses scale with how hard they attack; German losses with the pressure and their own strength.
  • Spring. Stocks carry forward with production (German output for the army raised; Soviet output lowered without Lend-Lease) and replacements. The Moscow offensive's pace is a Typhoon-like 20–30 km a day scaled by the force ratio and cut by the prepared defences.

The ranges cover uncertainty in the strength of each mechanism, not in the model's structure. Figures marked approximate in the source file (some autumn equipment losses, the Soviet field strengths, the spring 1942 stocks) are estimates. The model is a check on the scenario's internal consistency, not a forecast.
